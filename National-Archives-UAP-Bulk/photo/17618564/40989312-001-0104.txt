Chapter Twenty Five

The Woodrow Wilson Center and the AAAS
1971 - 1974

When Athelstan decided to leave Palm Beach, he was invited to be one of the first
Fellows at the newly established Woodrow Wilson International Center for Scholars in
Washington, D.C. There was no memorial in Washington to President Wilson, and Athel
liked the idea of one that combined scholarship and public service.

“That idea was really the origin of the Woodrow Wilson Center, and this center was
funded partly by Congress and partly by grants, much the way the Smithsonian funds are
raised. It was housed in the old Smithsonian Castle, where it still is. The Center gave me
a very nice, understandable reason to leave Florida. I wanted to come up and get in the
middle of things anyway. So I moved to Washington in January 1971, and had my office
there. The idea at the Center is to have about half Americans and half visiting foreigners,
and they really had some wonderful people. The first director was Benjamin Read, who
had been a high-ranking career official in the State Department.

“One Center scholar I remember with affection and humor was Rajeshwar Dayal from
India. After Indian Independence, he was Foreign Secretary, Ambassador to France and
Yugoslavia, and Permanent Representative of India to the United Nations. When he
arrived, I took him down to visit with Dillon Ripley, who was Secretary of the
Smithsonian and always very nice. We sat down to tea and he was talking about his
recent visit to India. Not knowing Dillon’s preoccupation as a conservationist, Dayal
said to him, “Well, next time you come to India, Mr. Ripley, you must let me know. Ill
take you on a tiger hunt. You know, there’s no danger. You go up in a howdah on an
elephant and ride there, and our beaters will get the tiger out and you shoot it.” It was a
funny situation, for Dayal did not know Ripley had devoted a lot of his life to the
preservation of the Bengal tiger. There was no harm on either side. Dayal knew better
than Ripley that these damned tigers eat people and ravage villages and are vermin to an
Indian. Only someone as dedicated to the preservation of wildlife as Ripley, would not
realize how he sounded when he said, “The hell with the eating of people, let’s conserve
the tigers.” One of the many interesting twists of two men of good will who approach
things from different points of view.
