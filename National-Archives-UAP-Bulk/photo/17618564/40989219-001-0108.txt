It will not be denied that Congress has the power to
establish rules for the government of the armed forces,
but it is submitted that that authority is limited by other
provisions of the Constitution.?
An examination of Article 134 indicates that three
general classes of conduct are prohibited. The first of
these is "all disorders and neglects to the prejudice of
good order and discipline in the armed forces." Does such
phraseology indicate specific offenses. It is so broad,
instead, that it encompasses scores, if not hundreds, of
offenses.
The second class of conduct prohibited is "all conduct
of a nature to bring discredit upon the armed forces." Again
the language is too general to lend itself to certainty
as to what is meant.
The third class of conduct proscribed is "crimes and
offenses, not capital.
•..", and again there is no certainty.
The fact is that the definition of the terms of the
Article which would, in turn, define the offenses en-
compassed by the Article, is left up to the court. Congress
did not define, with any degree of certainty, the specific
conduct which they intended to proscribe.
A statute is inherently void when it leaves to the
Court and jury, in a legislative capacity, the ascertain-
ment and fixing of the proscribed conduct, before the guilt
of the accused is established.10
It is therefore submitted that, because Article 134,
Uniform Code of Military Justice, does not fix an ascertain-
able standard of what conduct is proscribed, it is void for
uncertainty.
