Chapter Nine

The War Years
(1941 - 1945)

Spilhaus describes the tense atmosphere that existed at the Meteorology Institute at NYU
where he was training 1000 Cadets for the Navy. After the bombing of Pearl Harbor his
mission had changed and it became his duty to prepare his students for war. This
assignment was made more difficult by the status of his citizenship: he was still a British
subject.

“T couldn’t understand why the United States didn’t go in and help the British. That’s
when Columbus Iselin and I decided to leak the secrets of the BT to British Intelligence.
This could have been very tense for Columbus so I went to New York to carry out the
mission. When we arrived, we arranged to meet the British Naval Attache, a Captain in
the Royal Navy. We had to do this very discreetly, because this was a peculiar situa-
tion—America wasn’t in the war. I met the Attache in the apartment of a friend in Sutton
Place, Manhattan. It seems pretty funny now, but it was actually a very tricky business
for a Britisher like me, who'd been involved in secret sonar research, to be giving those
secrets to the British. It could have also caused great trouble for Woods Hole. I took the
responsibility without any qualms or consequences. I remember that old Naval Attache
saying, “Well, it will all go into the lion’s maw, and what will come out, we don’t yet
know.”

Dr. Spilhaus later learned that, as a result of this meeting, there was a request by the
British to President Roosevelt for 200 BTs. “That was well before the United States
entered the war. I didn’t know all the details, but I was delighted. Of course, I gave them
the rights to manufacture the Bathythermographs.” Athel soon became a soldier in
uniform himself. He describes what happened to the BT.

“I didn’t find out what went on until after the war. Then I found that nearly every damn
ship had one of my devices on it, so I collected a few royalties from the Navy because I
had patented the BT in 1937. As that was the first time I ever had any extra money, I
built a house on Cape Cod at Bourne with the royalties, thinking it would be a nice place
close to Woods Hole for summers, and near enough to NYU for the winters.

“As soon as World War II began, I went straight to Washington in early September 1939
when the Nazis invaded Poland. Technically, I was still in the South African Army and I
reported for duty at the South African Embassy. They told me I'd better remain a
professor and sent me back to NYU. With typical British complacency they said, “You'll
be more useful as a professor, and the U.S. will join us one day.”
