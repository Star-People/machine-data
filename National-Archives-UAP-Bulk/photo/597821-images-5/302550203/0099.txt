The vast majority of observers saw the
fireball as blue, green, or green-white over
most of its path, but a few reported a
change to yellow or orange towards the
end, after disintegration. Magnitude esti-
mates scatter from —4 to —18 because of
differences in distance to the path and
in observer training. Disregarding dis-
tance, the average of 33 estimates is —10.
Many observers compared the angular di-
ameter of the fireball with the moon’s,
their results ranging from 8’ to 30’.

Very near its end, the bolide was cap-
tured in three photographs by Veronica
Schwartz at Baldwinsville, New York,
which record successive stages of the dis-
integration. As the last picture on the
June, 1966, cover shows, fully a dozen
large fragments and many smaller ones
appear. This multiplicity added to the
difficulty of distant observers in fixing the
end point.

No reliable observer saw the whole
path and also estimated the time the
bolide took to traverse it. We sought to
calculate the average velocity through the
atmosphere by combining observed sector
and duration in the 20 cases where both
were recorded. The average velocity
comes out 23.5 + 6.6 kilometers per sec-
ond, and the overall duration 31 seconds.

Three orbits were calculated for the
meteoroid in space around the sun: el-

lipse, parabola, and hyperbola. These
correspond respectively to velocities of
88, 42, and 47 kilometers per second at
the earth's distance from the sun. The
corresponding velocities relative to the
earth are 12.5, 18, and 23.5 kilometers a
second, the last being the observed value.
However, if the actual orbit lies between
the elliptical and the parabolic cases, the
body might have come from the asteroidal
zone, since the orbital inclination to the
plane of the ecliptic is not too large.

After the fireball had passed through
the atmosphere, its train lasted so long
that a nonuniform drift would be ex-

pected. Photographs show that it did

not remain straight but appeared wavy,
the irregularities being small except near
the end. The train was compared in ap-
pearance to the trail left by a jet air-
plane, and many descriptions include the
word ‘‘smoke.”

Two observers, D. B. Smith (Platts-
burgh, New York) and D. K. Speed (Glas-
tonbury, Connecticut), charted both the
fireball and train on AMS maps. The
general drift of the train was westward,
but it developed humps that made the
direction of motion unclear. Four other
observers described the travel as to the
west, two to the east.

T. D. Nicholson (Woodcliff Lake, New
ersey) observed the train in much
detail. He noted its general drift as to
the west-northwest, and that it started
15° southwest of the zenith and was vis-

ible to 10° above the horizon, which was
formed by buildings. This report is sig-
nificant because many observers in Vir-
ginia, Maryland, and southern New Jer-
sey did not mention a train, and a few
stated specifically that there was none.
Most of these people saw only the first
part of the path.

From all the evidence, we infer that
the train (IT) began to be visible at a
height of 90 kilometers, about 200 kilo-
meters from the beginning point of the
path, and then extended in some form
or other to the end.

Just before this paper was completed,
H. Oliak’s very full discussion of the
fireball of January 14, 1965, over Poland
and Germany, appeared in Mitteilungen
27, Babelsberg Observatory. That fireball
also had a very long, almost horizontal
path, and its apparent magnitude was
—11. On various assumptions, he calculat-
ed a diameter from 0.6 to 1.5 meters
and a mass from 500 to 6,000 kilograms.
The analogy suggests that the April 25th
bolide was quite a massive body too,
though somewhat smaller, and that it
should have dropped meteorites. The
debris, if any, would have fallen on Ca-
nadian territory, and for this reason P. M.
Milliman of Ottawa has also made a de-
tailed derivation of the path.
